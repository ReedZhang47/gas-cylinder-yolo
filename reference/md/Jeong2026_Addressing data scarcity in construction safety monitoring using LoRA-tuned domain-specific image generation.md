## Abstract

This paper proposes a lightweight domain adaptation framework for construction safety monitoring by fine-tuning a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA). To simulate high-risk construction environments underrepresented in training data, the model was adapted to environmental features and specific hazards, focusing on visually dominant scenarios including falls, struck-by, and caught-in incidents. To address data scarcity, Multi-LoRA fine-tuning was conducted using 20 images per hazard type (totaling 60 across three hazards) and 30 background images, enabling both contextual and hazard-specific adaptation. The generated images achieved the highest semantic consistency, yielding the top mean Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training (CLIP) scores with minimal variance, and improved visual realism by reducing the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) by 86.72 points. Furthermore, a YOLOv8 model trained exclusively on these synthetic images achieved a mean average precision (mAP@0.5:0.95) of 94.1% on real-world frames, comparable to a baseline model trained on real data.

## 1. Introduction

Ensuring safety and implementing effective hazard management on construction sites are critical concerns that directly impact the occupational safety of on-site workers and the overall success of construction projects. The inherently dynamic and complex nature of construction sites, as opposed to more controlled industrial environments, makes safety management significantly more difficult and unpredictable [1–3]. The construction industry consistently ranks among the most hazardous sectors, with thousands of fatalities reported each year [4,5]. The underlying causes are largely attributable to highly variable site conditions, including shifting work zones, the involvement of multiple subcontractors, and heavy equipment operations, as well as environmental factors such as weather, site accessibility, and outdoor constraints (e.g., limited visibility, uneven terrain, and extreme temperatures) [6]. To mitigate these risks, construction sites typically assign designated safety personnel responsible for frequent site inspections, hazard monitoring, and enforcement of proper safety standards [7]. However, the shortage of qualified safety managers, combined with limited time and financial resources, often makes it impractical to comprehensively oversee all construction activities, thereby hindering effective site-level safety oversight and highlighting the need for automated, scalable monitoring systems capable of operating under data-limited conditions [8,9]. Motivated by these limitations, researchers have begun to adopt artificial intelligence (AI)-based technologies for automating safety management [10,11], with computer vision emerging as a particularly promising solution due to its scalability, cost-efficiency, and superior ability to detect and assess safety-related events, such as personal protective equipment (PPE) compliance and hazardous behaviors, from visual data in highly unstructured and unpredictable site environments [12–15].

To enable effective vision-based monitoring on construction sites, object detection serves as a foundational computer vision task and is one of the most widely utilized in real-world applications [16]. However, the lack of high-quality accident datasets presents a significant challenge in achieving high accuracy with state-of-the-art models such as YOLOv8 [17] and YOLOv11 [18]. Collecting real-world visual data on construction hazards poses ethical and legal challenges, as these incidents are unpredictable, hazardous to replicate, and rarely captured on camera [19]. Moreover, these events are rarely included in publicly available datasets, and reproducing them for data collection is neither feasible nor safe [20,21]. In response to these limitations, researchers have increasingly explored synthetic image generation as a practical alternative to real-world data acquisition. Efforts to address these difficulties have included the use of web crawling techniques to collect construction site images that supplement vision-based model training [22]. Additionally, game engines have been utilized to generate synthetic datasets, for example, to train models for activity recognition in construction environments [23], or to simulate hazardous scenarios for safety monitoring purposes [24]. More recently, the advent of generative diffusion models has introduced a promising new approach for data generation. Models such as DALL⋅E [25], Stable Diffusion [26,27], and Flux.1 [28] have attracted considerable attention for their ability to generate high-quality, contextually rich images from textual prompts, opening new possibilities for their application in computer vision tasks. Accordingly, preliminary investigations have demonstrated the feasibility of text-to-image models to generate synthetic representations of construction hazards from domain-specific textual inputs [29].

Generating effective synthetic training data for object detection remains challenging, especially for specialized applications in construction safety. Since most text-to-image models are trained on general-purpose datasets like LAION [30], they often have limited capacity to represent site-specific characteristics, especially those critical for safety in construction contexts. The domain gap can compromise the realism of generated images, particularly in depicting hazardous scenarios, intricate interactions among workers, equipment, and materials, and the distinctive visual characteristics of fixed-view construction footage, such as lighting conditions and typical background clutter present in outdoor environments. To better capture the inherently complex and dynamic characteristics of construction environments, prompts often need to include multiple subject categories. Including multiple subject categories often leads to a phenomenon known as concept bleeding, where visually distinct objects (e.g., workers, equipment, materials) become fused together or distorted in unrealistic ways, resulting in ambiguous outputs that fail to align with the intended scene structure and pseudo-labels [31]. Furthermore, even when specific objects are clearly described in the prompt, some may not appear in the generated output, leading to incomplete or missing foreground elements. These generation errors undermine the consistency and reliability of synthetic images, ultimately limiting their effectiveness as training data for object detection in safety-critical construction applications. Consequently, the performance of object detection systems in construction safety applications can be hindered, causing inaccurate hazard detection and unreliable monitoring outcomes.

In this regard, this study proposes a Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)-tuned, domain-aware image generation pipeline that (1) fine-tunes a diffusion model on field data, (2) generates realistic training images by combining construction-specific backgrounds with safety-critical scenarios, and (3) demonstrates the applicability of the resulting dataset for object detection in real-world construction environments. Leveraging LoRA's parameter-efficient design, the method allows fine-tuning with limited data, mitigates overfitting, and supports modular adaptation to specific safety contexts. Flux.1 was chosen as the base model due to its demonstrated ability to generate high-quality, photorealistic images with strong spatial consistency, which is crucial for replicating realistic construction environments. To account for the distinctive characteristics of construction scenes, the fine-tuning process adopts a Multi-LoRA structure in which background features and hazard semantics are learned separately using dedicated LoRA modules. The background module captures environmental features commonly observed in construction surveillance imagery, such as fixed camera angles, varying lighting conditions, and visual clutter resulting from ongoing activities and structural elements. The hazard-specific LoRA focuses on three major incident types (i.e., falls, struck-by, and caught-in) identified by the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) as the leading causes of on-site injuries [32]. These categories were selected due to their visually observable characteristics in typical construction footage, making them suitable for deep learning model training. Although electrocution is recognized as one of OSHA's "Fatal Four" leading causes of fatalities, it was excluded from this study because distinct visual cues, such as visible electrical arcs or direct contact with power sources, are rarely visible in standard surveillance imagery. Moreover, the semantic ambiguity and scarcity of high-quality training data further complicate the modeling of electrocution scenarios using vision-based approaches. Therefore, this study focused on hazards that are more visually distinguishable and structurally suited to current generative and detection pipelines. By embedding safety-relevant situational concepts for the selected hazards, the proposed framework enables the scalable generation of realistic safety imagery that enhances the accuracy and robustness of vision-based hazard detection systems in real construction environments.

## 2. Literature review

This section reviews existing studies on vision-based safety monitoring at construction sites. It first examines computer vision technologies used to detect construction-related objects, such as workers and equipment, and to analyze unsafe actions or behaviors from visual data in order to identify potential safety risks. The section then reviews domain-specific synthetic image generation approaches as compelling alternatives to conventional data collection methods, addressing the significant data acquisition challenges inherent in construction environments. These approaches overcome the difficulties of capturing the dynamic and complex visual characteristics of construction sites while providing sufficient training data for robust object detection models.

### 2.1. Object detection for vision-based site monitoring in construction

Computer vision technologies have been increasingly utilized to improve safety monitoring practices on construction sites. The core objective of such systems is threefold: (1) to detect construction objects (i.e., workers, equipment, materials), (2) to analyze unsafe actions or behaviors (e.g., missing PPE, unsafe postures, or proximity to heavy machinery) from visual data, and (3) to identify potential risks. With respect to these functions, object detection serves as a critical component, as it provides essential contextual information while also acting as a key input for downstream visual data-centric tasks such as interaction and activity recognition, both of which are vital for monitoring and managing hazardous situations. Specifically, object detection forms the foundation for vision-based safety monitoring tasks; for instance, it performs PPE compliance verification by detecting helmets and vests [33,34]. To ensure reliable detection in real-world conditions, YOLO-based models improve recognition of small-scale PPE (e.g., helmets, vests) in cluttered environments [35], while face detection with deep transfer learning maintains accuracy under occlusion and scale variation [36]. Beyond individual object detection, this technology plays a critical role in monitoring interactions between workers and their surroundings. It supports contextual interaction analysis by localizing entities [37], enables lightweight, real-time implementation on embedded systems [38], and contributes to model robustness through large-scale annotated datasets [39]. Spatial zone-related hazard monitoring also relies on object detection to capture proximity relationships between workers and environmental risks, such as identifying missing fall protection in elevated areas or assessing proximity to openings and drop-offs from surveillance imagery [40,41]. Transformer-based methods further support this task by localizing spatial boundaries under occlusion and distortion, where accurate detection remains critical for risk classification [42].

While computer vision-based object detection has shown considerable potential for enhancing construction safety monitoring, its effectiveness fundamentally depends on the availability of high-quality, domain-specific training data [43]. The inherently dynamic and complex nature of construction environments poses significant challenges for developing robust detection models. Site conditions constantly change with varying lighting, weather, viewpoints, and object appearances, resulting in temporal dynamics that alter the visual landscape, congested environments with background clutter and frequent occlusions that complicate reliable tracking, and reduced performance in action recognition when workers or equipment are partially hidden from view [44–46]. Furthermore, safety-critical data collection is particularly problematic due to ethical and legal considerations, as capturing actual safety violations or near-miss incidents for training data raises ethical concerns, while reproducing hazardous scenarios for data collection purposes is neither feasible nor safe [19]. These data acquisition challenges significantly impact model performance, leading to reduced accuracy and reliability in real-world construction settings [47]. Traditional approaches to address data limitations have focused on data augmentation techniques [48]; however, these methods often struggle to represent the contextual richness and complex spatial relationships characteristic of construction environments. Similarly, semi-supervised learning approaches have been explored to reduce the dependency on labeled data [12], yet these techniques still face limitations in handling the diverse conditions encountered across different construction sites [24]. Even state-of-the-art models experience performance degradation when deployed in real-world construction scenarios, primarily due to the mismatch between training data distributions and the highly variable conditions encountered on actual construction sites [37,49]. To overcome these limitations, recent research has explored data-efficient learning strategies tailored to construction environments. These include multiscale object detection under limited supervision [50], task-aware meta-learning paradigms for structural damage segmentation using few annotated images [51], and transformer-based universal segmentation models optimized for generalization across damage types [52]. Collectively, these approaches demonstrate the potential of lightweight and adaptable vision models in data-scarce scenarios. Nevertheless, persistent challenges remain in achieving domain-specific realism and interpretability, particularly for safety-critical tasks requiring fine-grained hazard understanding.

### 2.2. Domain-aware synthetic image generation for object detection

To address the significant data acquisition challenges inherent in construction environments, domain-specific synthetic image generation approaches have emerged as compelling alternatives to conventional data collection. These methods overcome the difficulties of capturing dynamic and complex visual characteristics of construction sites while providing sufficient training data for robust object detection models. Web-crawled construction imagery represents an initial approach that preserves domain-specific features while circumventing real-world data collection challenges, enabling automated construction of large, high-quality training databases in minimal time [22]. Similarly, virtual reality technologies have been explored to simulate construction environments for various applications including emergency management [20]. Recent advancements in synthetic image dataset development have demonstrated that models trained on artificially generated construction equipment imagery can achieve high detection precision while significantly reducing data collection time and effort [53], and virtual reality-based synthetic data has been successfully applied to train models for tower crane operation monitoring in scenarios difficult to capture in real environments [23]. The simulation of different lighting, weather, and viewpoint conditions has helped address environmental variability challenges, with game engine-driven synthetic data generation substantially improving detection performance for safety monitoring applications [24].

The emergence of generative AI, pioneered by DALL-E [25], revolutionized image synthesis by enabling the creation of contextually rich visuals from text prompts, while Stable Diffusion [26] substantially improved this capability through its efficient latent diffusion approach. SDXL [27] enhanced image quality with dual text encoders and a larger model size, delivering superior composition and text rendering capabilities. Concurrently, Google's Imagen [54] achieved exceptional photorealism through its cascaded diffusion architecture, particularly excelling at complex spatial relationships and intricate details. Most recently, Flux.1 [28] has set new standards in photorealistic generation with superior handling of lighting, skin textures, and anatomical accuracy. Despite significant advancements in text-to-image generation models, domain-specific applications remain challenging due to their reliance on general datasets like LAION-5B [30]. This dataset predominantly contains general-purpose imagery, lacking sufficient representation of specialized concepts and intricate visual relationships required by specific application domains. To address this limitation, researchers have developed various domain adaptation approaches. DreamBooth [55] enables personalized image synthesis by fine-tuning a diffusion model with a small set of images and text prompts associated with a unique identifier. It achieves impressive fidelity in capturing subject-specific visual features but often requires full-model tuning and careful regularization to avoid overfitting or identity leakage, which can be resource-intensive in constrained settings. The encoder-based domain tuning technique [56] accelerates personalization dramatically using just a single reference image and minimal training steps through a domain-specific encoder paired with regularized weight-offsets. In the architectural domain, a multi-network combined approach for building facade image generation [57] demonstrated that Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) significantly reduces the complexity of fine-tuning large models while ControlNet integration enhances output controllability. The ODGEN framework [31] generates high-quality training data for domain-specific object detection, demonstrating significant improvements in performance metrics. Investigations into medical imaging applications [58] have revealed crucial trade-offs between accuracy and interpretability in fine-tuned diffusion models.

In line with these advancements in domain adaptation, the construction field has also begun to explore text-to-image generation for synthetic data creation, though without adapting the generative models themselves to the domain. Recent research has demonstrated that standard Stable Diffusion can generate construction task images with reasonable fidelity, with a substantial majority of synthesized images deemed suitable for representation, and Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) models trained on these images achieving high classification accuracies [59]. Similarly, systematic prompt engineering strategies have been developed to generate thousands of images across numerous hazardous scenarios, with Deep Neural Network (DNN) models trained on these synthetic images achieving promising results in both object detection and segmentation tasks [29]. However, these studies primarily rely on frozen, general-purpose models without modifying the underlying weights. Consequently, they often struggle to capture the nuances of construction-specific textures and site layouts, limiting their ability to consistently generate physically plausible hazard scenarios. These limitations include common generation failures like concept bleeding where distinct objects appear fused together, inconsistent visual quality, and missing foreground instances despite their specification in prompts [60,61]. Despite their growing use, most text-to-image approaches rely on generic models without domain-specific adaptation, limiting their ability to capture the nuanced visual characteristics of construction environments. Correspondingly, systematic evaluations of generation quality tailored to construction-specific requirements remain scarce [19,37]. Given the dynamic, risk-sensitive nature of construction sites, domain adaptation for safety monitoring must be both responsive to site-specific visual cues and deployable under practical constraints.

Traditional full-model fine-tuning approaches such as DreamBooth or ControlNet, while powerful, often require substantial computational resources, additional conditional annotations for spatial control, and long training cycles. These demands are often incompatible with the resource-constrained and time-sensitive environments typical of real-world construction projects. Furthermore, they typically necessitate the manual preparation of additional conditional annotations (e.g., depth maps or Canny edges) to maintain spatial layout, which is often impractical in time-sensitive and resource-constrained construction environments. Therefore, more lightweight and modular approaches to domain tuning are needed to balance adaptability with feasibility. Addressing these challenges requires a more sophisticated approach to domain-specific image generation for construction safety applications. Parameter-efficient techniques like LoRA are particularly relevant in this context, as they enable effective model adaptation with limited training data while maintaining flexibility. Recent findings from domain adaptation in the medical field, where data scarcity and high semantic precision are equally critical, indicate that LoRA can perform on par with, or even surpass, full fine-tuning. This underscores its strong applicability to construction safety tasks facing similar constraints [62]. Targeted domain adaptation could improve text-to-image quality for training object detection systems while providing better understanding of synthetic data characteristics in construction-specific contexts, ultimately enhancing safety monitoring capabilities while addressing data scarcity challenges. In response to these challenges, this paper proposes a solution: a domain-adaptive image generation framework based on LoRA, specifically designed to address the visual and contextual complexities of construction safety applications. By independently fine-tuning background and hazard-related semantics through dedicated LoRA modules, the proposed approach facilitates modular and scalable synthesis of high-fidelity training images in data-constrained settings. This structure enhances visual realism and semantic consistency, thereby improving the effectiveness of downstream object detection tasks in safety-critical construction environments.

## 3. Methodology

This research aims to generate domain-specific artificial data tailored for construction safety monitoring. Building upon the capabilities of Flux.1-dev [28], which demonstrates superior performance among open-source text-to-image models and crucially supports fine-tuning capabilities, this study focuses on three major hazard types (i.e., falls, struck-by, and caught-in incidents) that represent the highest-frequency accidents according to OSHA statistics [32] while being visually distinguishable in surveillance footage. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the methodology consists of three main steps: (1) data curation and preprocessing, where construction closed-circuit television (CCTV) footage and hazard scenario data are collected and processed through image resizing and text tagging, (2) LoRA fine-tuning process utilizing preprocessed image-text pairs to train Background and Hazard-Specific LoRA modules, and (3) implementation of the generation workflow by loading pretrained components, applying Multi-LoRA, and performing sampling and variational autoencoder (VAE) decoding to produce domain-specific synthetic images that visualize construction site safety hazards.

### 3.1. Initial data curation and preprocessing strategy

Addressing the challenges in acquiring real-world safety incident data, a specialized dataset curation strategy was implemented. The collection prioritized capturing both the distinct visual characteristics of construction environments from surveillance cameras and representative examples of high-risk safety scenarios. Two primary image types were sourced: Background Context Images gathered from actual construction site surveillance video, representing diverse site layouts, lighting conditions, and camera perspectives; and Hazard Scenario Images depicting critical, visually distinguishable safety hazards relevant to the construction domain. Due to the rarity and danger of actual incidents, this hazard subset consisted of ethically sourced real images obtained through web crawling to ensure visual representation without hazardous replication. Representative samples from this curated dataset are illustrated in Fig. 2.

A standardized preprocessing pipeline ensured consistency for fine-tuning. All collected images were resized to a uniform 768 × 768 pixel resolution using aspect-ratio-preserving padding with black pixels to prevent geometric distortions detrimental to object detection training. Associated with each image, caption files provided textual conditioning through structured descriptive tags. These tags included general context descriptors like construction site or CCTV viewpoint, specific object and hazard labels generated through a combination of automated analysis derived from image features and filenames, and manual input via descriptive prompts tailored to the depicted scenario (e.g., worker near edge, excavator swing hazard) and quality or style modifiers (e.g., photorealistic, sharp focus) intended to guide generation fidelity. To streamline the training process, corresponding text embeddings derived from these captions and latent images encoded at this target resolution were precomputed. These textual descriptions, alongside the precomputed representations, served as the conditioning inputs during the LoRA fine-tuning phase, guiding the model toward generating high-fidelity outputs relevant to construction safety.

### 3.2. LoRA fine-tuning architecture and process

To address the practical challenges of construction safety monitoring such as limited high-quality data, fast deployment needs, and restricted computing resources, this study employs the parameter-efficient fine-tuning technique, LoRA [63], to adapt a pre-trained text-to-image model. While state-of-the-art alternatives offer advanced controllability, they typically require full-model fine-tuning and large computational budgets, which are often incompatible with deployment-focused scenarios. The selection of LoRA reflects a deliberate trade-off between strict structural controllability and resource-efficient scalability. Unlike standard structural control methods that demand extra conditional annotations (e.g., depth maps) and substantial overhead, the proposed framework prioritizes a lightweight adaptation strategy to enable rapid deployment using minimal site-specific data. LoRA is particularly advantageous when working with limited domain-specific datasets, as it mitigates catastrophic forgetting of the base model's capabilities and supports modular adaptation strategies for tasks like style transfer or specific concept learning [64,65], applied here to the Flux.1-dev base model.

The core principle of LoRA involves injecting trainable, low-rank matrices into specific layers, typically the attention mechanism layers, of the frozen pre-trained model weights (W₀). Instead of updating W₀ directly, LoRA learns low-rank updates (ΔW) decomposed into two smaller matrices, A and B (A ∈ R^(d×r), B ∈ R^(r×k), with rank r ≪ min(d, k)), such that ΔW = BA. The adapted weight W′ is computed as W′ = W₀ + (α/r)·ΔW, where α is a scaling factor. This approach significantly reduces the number of trainable parameters. Implemented using established training scripts [66], LoRA adaptation specifically targeted the linear projection layers for Query (Q), Key (K), and Value (V) within the attention mechanisms of the Flux.1-dev U-Net component, which is illustrated in Fig. 3 as the core update mechanism. This targeting is a common practice, as the U-Net, which heavily influences the visual output in diffusion models, is fine-tuned while the parameters of the text encoders and standard convolutional layers remain frozen. The process utilized the L1 Loss function, suitable for preserving sharp details in image generation, and employed BF16 mixed precision for computational efficiency. Technical optimizations like gradient checkpointing and caching strategies were implemented using the aforementioned training scripts.

A modular training strategy involving two distinct LoRA types was adopted. First, a Background LoRA was trained using only the curated background context images and associated general environmental tags. This module aims to capture the stylistic features of construction site surveillance imagery (e.g., typical viewpoints, lighting). Second, separate Hazard-Specific LoRAs were trained individually for the major hazard categories of falls, struck-by, and caught-in, using the corresponding hazard scenario images and specific descriptive tags. This specialization enables each LoRA to learn the unique visual semantics of a particular hazard, effectively decoupling background style from hazard details and allowing flexible combination during image synthesis [67,68]. Detailed hyperparameters are provided in the experimental setup section.

### 3.3. Domain-specific image generation workflow and evaluation setup

This section details the workflow designed for generating domain-specific synthetic images tailored for construction safety monitoring, along with the assessment of their utility for downstream object detection tasks. Leveraging a node-based graphical interface system [69], the workflow utilizes the domain-tuned LoRA modules to synthesize targeted hazard scenarios. The process encompasses prompt engineering with Multi-LoRA conditioning, specific sampling configurations, and a curation process for the generated outputs, the overall structure of which is depicted in Fig. 4. Finally, the setup for evaluating the effectiveness of the resulting synthetic dataset using a downstream object detection task is described.

#### 3.3.1. Prompt engineering and multi-LoRA conditioning

The image synthesis process was guided by carefully formulated positive text prompts describing the target scene composition. These prompts described the target scene, incorporating keywords like construction site to activate the Background LoRA for environmental context, and additional phrases such as dangerous proximity, caught in steel beam, or fall from height to activate the relevant Hazard-Specific LoRA for depicting the particular incident. For instance, prompts such as "a worker working on high scaffolding", "he falls from height", "construction site", and "no lanyard connected to safety harness" were used to generate fall hazard scenarios, combining the general site context with specific fall-related descriptors. A key feature of the workflow was the simultaneous application and weighting of multiple LoRA modules during the diffusion sampling process. Within the node-based interface, this involved loading the core components: the base Flux.1-dev model, its associated VAE, and the text encoders contrastive language-image pre-training (CLIP) [70] and text-to-text transfer transformer [71], which provide the necessary multimodal conditioning signals. Dedicated nodes were used to load the trained Background LoRA and one Hazard-Specific LoRA. The outputs of these LoRA loading nodes were integrated into the model components, primarily the U-Net, facilitating a simultaneous Multi-LoRA blending process during the single-pass diffusion sampling. Crucially, adjustable strength parameters within these nodes allowed fine-grained control over the influence of each LoRA module. These weights were empirically optimized based on the visual nature of each hazard type. For 'Falls', where actor pose is critical, the research prioritized the Hazard LoRA (1.2) over the Background (0.8) to prevent clutter. For 'Struck-by', which relies on the interaction between workers and equipment, a balanced high-strength configuration (Background 0.9, Hazard 1.2) was used. Conversely, 'Caught-in' scenarios, where the environmental structure itself constitutes the risk, increased the Background strength to 1.0 (with Hazard at 1.1) to ensure the surrounding context was densely verified. This adaptive weighting ensured that safety-critical elements were rendered with high fidelity while maintaining the appropriate level of contextual dominance. These positive prompts were processed through text encoding nodes connected appropriately within the LoRA-modified model chain. Specialized nodes for adjusting the guidance mechanism specific to the Flux.1 architecture were also incorporated to refine prompt adherence, as negative prompts were not employed in this workflow. This simultaneous Multi-LoRA blending strategy ensured that background and hazard features were synthesized concurrently, avoiding the need for a sequential or multi-stage generation pipeline.

#### 3.3.2. Sampling strategy configuration

The core generative step, executed within a sampler node in the pipeline, employed a specific configuration to control the diffusion denoising process. The chosen sampler, Denoising Exponential Integrator Sampler, known for efficient generation, and the SGM-Uniform scheduler, which employs a uniform timestep spacing strategy, dictated the trajectory through the noise levels during iterative denoising. Flux.1 utilizes a distinct guidance mechanism compared to traditional classifier-free guidance; its strength was controlled primarily through the positive prompt structure and specialized guidance adjustment nodes (e.g., a FluxGuidance node with a fixed strength setting) within the workflow, as negative prompts were not employed. An initial latent tensor corresponding to the target 1344 × 768 resolution served as the starting point; this resolution was chosen to better approximate the wide aspect ratio, similar to the standard 16:9, commonly observed in CCTV surveillance footage, thereby enhancing the contextual realism for the intended application domain. To ensure diversity in the generated dataset, the random seed controlling the initial noise and sampling variations was systematically varied across generation batches for identical prompt and LoRA configurations. The final denoised latent representation was then decoded into a pixel-space image using the VAE decoder node.

#### 3.3.3. Generation output curation process

Recognizing that even fine-tuned generative models can produce low-fidelity or semantically misaligned outputs, this study implemented a qualitative review process for the synthesized images. This curation step was crucial for ensuring the utility of the synthetic data in training downstream object detectors. Each generated image was inspected based on several criteria, including the absence of significant visual artifacts (e.g., distortions, unnatural textures), overall coherence and logical consistency of the depicted scene, fidelity to the input text prompt and the intended semantic content targeted by the active LoRAs, integrity of depicted objects while avoiding severe concept bleeding or unrealistic fusion, and clear and unambiguous representation of the intended construction safety hazard. Images failing to meet these quality standards were discarded. As illustrated in Fig. 5, the curation process applied lightweight but consistent quality control criteria to filter out visually implausible outputs. These included critical rendering errors (e.g., missing limbs or broken geometry), prompt misalignment (e.g., incorrect object-action correspondence), unrealistic object fusion (e.g., entangled excavator arms), and scale distortion (e.g., human figures disproportionately larger than construction equipment). Rather than relying on downstream model performance to filter training data, this step was designed to reflect real-world deployment scenarios, where obviously flawed images would undermine data usability. While inherently qualitative, the applied criteria were grounded in observable physical inconsistencies and were applied consistently across the dataset. In addition to this qualitative review, a lightweight CLIP-based evaluation module was incorporated into the image generation pipeline to provide an objective screening layer. For each synthesized image, the CLIP similarity score between the generated output and its corresponding text prompt was automatically computed at the final stage of generation. Empirically, samples with CLIP similarity scores in the lower tail of the distribution, approximately 1–1.5 standard deviations below the mean, roughly corresponding to 24.0 on the experimental scale, consistently exhibited prompt inconsistencies or visual artifacts and were therefore filtered out during preprocessing.

#### 3.3.4. Validating synthetic data effectiveness for object detection training

To validate the effectiveness of the generated synthetic dataset as training data, its impact on training an object detection model was evaluated. Specifically, a YOLOv8 object detection model [72] was trained exclusively on the curated synthetic images produced by the previously described workflow. The model was trained to detect key entities commonly involved in construction site hazards, specifically worker, excavator, and steel beam. The performance of this synthetically trained model in relevant construction site scenarios was then measured using the standard mean average precision (mAP) metric, specifically mAP@0.5:0.95, which averages precision across multiple intersection over union (IoU) thresholds from 0.5 to 0.95. This evaluation quantifies the effectiveness of the synthetic data by measuring the performance of a model trained solely on it for the intended object detection task.

## 4. Experiments

To examine the effectiveness of the LoRA-tuned domain-specific image generation approach, a series of experiments were conducted, focusing on semantic alignment, visual realism, and downstream applicability. The evaluation includes quantitative assessments of image quality and semantic consistency, as well as an analysis of the generated data's utility in enhancing the performance of downstream object detection tasks relevant to construction safety monitoring. The datasets used for training and evaluation and key implementation details are described, followed by results from image quality and object detection experiments, with evaluation metrics integrated into each section.

### 4.1. Experimental datasets and implementation details

To fine-tune the Hazard-Specific LoRA modules, 60 images depicting safety-critical scenarios, 20 for each target category (falls, struck-by, and caught-in), were collected via web crawling. These images were selected based on their clarity in illustrating the respective hazard types, irrespective of scene context, and were sourced independently of the construction sites used for background context. In contrast, the Background LoRA module was trained using images curated from three distinct construction sites: one involving open-area excavation and earthwork operations, another focused on structural steel framing above ground with high worker density and active movement, and the third centered on dense substructure assembly with embedded steel elements. From each site, 10 representative background images (30 in total) were extracted to reflect typical environmental and operational contexts. Additionally, 1000 CCTV frames per site (3000 in total) were reserved for evaluating downstream object detection performance; to prevent data leakage, all evaluation frames were drawn from non-overlapping camera views within the same sites.

Both the Background and Hazard-Specific LoRA modules were fine-tuned using a parameter-efficient configuration based on the Flux.1-dev model. Due to the greater contextual complexity required for background modeling, the Background LoRA module was trained for 10 epochs, whereas the Hazard-Specific LoRA modules were fine-tuned for 5 epochs. The object detection model was trained exclusively on synthetic images generated through the domain-specific pipeline that combined the Background LoRA and the corresponding Hazard-Specific LoRA modules. All generated images underwent manual quality control to ensure semantic fidelity with the intended prompts. Synthesized outputs exhibiting object omission, visual artifacts, or conceptual inconsistencies were excluded, resulting in a high-quality synthetic dataset optimized for training. As the object classes in synthetic images were predefined through prompt design, annotations were inherently derived from the prompt definitions, ensuring semantic alignment without manual labeling errors. In contrast, the real-world test dataset consisting of 3000 frames was independently annotated without prior knowledge of object presence, thereby ensuring an unbiased benchmark for evaluating model performance.

All training and inference procedures were conducted on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU. Each Hazard-Specific LoRA module was trained using approximately 30 GB of memory over a period of six hours, based on 20 images per hazard type. The Background LoRA module, trained separately on 30 background-only images, required approximately eight hours under similar conditions. Image generation was performed using approximately 24 GB of memory, with each sample taking around 30 s to produce. Compared to full-model fine-tuning of the 12-billion-parameter Flux.1-dev model, which would typically exceed 90 GB of memory and require updating all parameters, the LoRA adaptation trains less than 1% of the model weights, resulting in roughly a 100- to 200-fold reduction in trainable parameters while achieving comparable domain adaptability. Since data generation and model adaptation are performed periodically rather than continuously, and the model can be updated without full retraining, the computational requirements remain lightweight and well-suited for deployment in real-world construction safety monitoring pipelines.

### 4.2. Semantic consistency evaluation using CLIP score

To assess how faithfully the generated images reflect the intended semantic content described in text prompts, particularly for safety-critical construction hazard scenarios, the CLIP score was employed [70]. This metric computes the cosine similarity between embeddings obtained from the CLIP image encoder for a generated image and embeddings from the CLIP text encoder for the corresponding input prompt. Specifically, the CLIP score is defined as s(t, i) = (t·i) / (‖t‖·‖i‖), where t and i represent the text and image feature vectors extracted from the CLIP encoders. [Note: the equation image was not extracted in the PDF conversion; the standard cosine-similarity form is restored here based on the surrounding text and [70].] The score quantifies the semantic similarity between the two modalities, with higher values indicating stronger alignment. Such alignment is essential for ensuring that synthetic images meaningfully reflect the intended textual scenarios, thereby enhancing their effectiveness as training data for downstream tasks such as object detection. The CLIP-ViT-L/14 [73] model was adopted for both the image and text encoders in this study.

To evaluate the effect of different LoRA conditioning strategies on semantic alignment, images were generated for each of the three hazard categories (i.e., fall, struck-by, and caught-in) under four configurations: (a) the Base Model (Flux.1-dev without any LoRA), (b) Background LoRA Only, (c) Hazard-Specific LoRA Only, and (d) Combined LoRAs (both Background and Hazard-Specific LoRAs applied with balanced weights).

For each condition, 1000 quality-filtered images were selected based on the same criteria applied during training data preparation, resulting in a total of 12,000 evaluation samples (i.e., 3 hazard types × 4 conditions × 1000 images each). CLIP scores were computed for each image using its generation prompt, and both the mean and standard deviation were calculated for each condition. The results are summarized in Table 2.

### 4.3. Visual realism via FID evaluation

To assess the visual fidelity and realism of generated synthetic images against real construction site CCTV footage, the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) was utilized [74]. FID scores were computed by measuring the Fréchet distance between the feature statistics (i.e., mean and covariance) of the real images and those of each generated set. The FID is defined as FID = ‖μr − μg‖² + Tr(Σr + Σg − 2(Σr·Σg)^(1/2)), where μr, Σr, and μg, Σg denote the means and covariances of the Inception features extracted from the real and generated image sets, respectively. [Note: the equation image was not extracted in the PDF conversion; the standard FID definition is restored here based on the surrounding text and [74].] This evaluation employed a pre-trained Inceptionv3 model [75] to extract features from images, following the standard FID computation protocol. Specifically, images were resized to 299 × 299 pixels and normalized before being processed through the model, with the final classification layer removed to obtain activation features.

Consistent with the preceding evaluation setup, the mean and covariance matrices of these features were calculated for three hazard types (i.e., falls, struck-by, and caught-in) under four generation conditions: (a) Base Model (Flux.1-dev without LoRA), (b) Background LoRA Only, (c) Hazard-Specific LoRA Only, and (d) Combined LoRAs (Background + Hazard). For each condition and hazard type, 1000 quality-filtered images, previously evaluated for CLIP scores, were compared against the corresponding real-image distribution derived from a reference test set of 3000 real-world construction site CCTV frames. The effectiveness of the proposed synthetic data generation framework was ultimately evaluated through its ability to train a high-performing object detection model. Specifically, a YOLOv8-m object detector was trained exclusively on the curated synthetic dataset of 3000 high-quality images, which had already undergone prior quantitative and qualitative validation. These images, evenly distributed across three targeted construction hazard categories (i.e., falls, struck-by, and caught-in) were generated through the combined application of the Background and Hazard-Specific LoRA modules, resulting in context-rich depictions of safety-critical scenes in the style of real-world construction surveillance footage. The object detection task focused on three classes essential to hazard monitoring in construction environments: "worker," "excavator," and "steel beam." ## 5. Discussion

The experimental results clearly demonstrate the effectiveness of the Combined LoRAs-based image generation strategy for constructing high-quality synthetic datasets tailored to construction safety applications. Across all evaluations, semantic consistency (CLIP), visual realism (FID), and downstream task performance (object detection), the combined use of both Background and Hazard-Specific LoRA modules consistently outperformed alternative configurations, including single-module variants and the unmodified base model. This consistent superiority stems from the modular decomposition of style and semantics: the Background LoRA successfully models the visual context of real-world construction surveillance (e.g., lighting, background clutter, and camera perspectives), while the Hazard-Specific LoRA ensures that the critical elements of each hazard scenario, such as falling workers, moving excavators, or entrapment risks, are represented with high semantic fidelity. Notably, the combined configuration yielded the highest CLIP scores with the lowest variance, indicating not only better average semantic alignment but also more stable prompt adherence across samples. Similarly, the lowest FID scores in this configuration suggest a closer match to the distribution of real-world CCTV imagery, further validating the visual credibility of the generated data. Despite their different training setups, the three single-LoRA baselines (i–iii) yielded visually similar outputs under identical prompts and seeds, indicating lack of diversity across configurations that narrows contextual variety and reduces controllability. This also coincided with over-smoothed textures and shading inconsistencies that diminish realistic construction-site cues (e.g., concrete/soil granularity, weathered metal, scaffold clutter). By contrast, the Multi-LoRA architecture preserved sharper object boundaries, richer background micro-structure, and a more realistic material feel of construction scenes, which is consistent with its lower FID and higher, more stable CLIP alignment, and it translated into stronger downstream detection when those images were used for training. Beyond statistical metrics, this improvement is closely linked to the physical plausibility of the Multi-LoRA outputs. Unlike sequential image-level composition, the simultaneous blending approach ensures photometric coherence, where the direction and intensity of shadows on hazard objects are naturally influenced by the ambient lighting of the site environment. As shown in Figs. 6 and 10, the synthesized workers and equipment exhibit shadows that align with the background's light source, maintaining high geometric and lighting consistency. This prevents the detector from learning irrelevant visual artifacts or depth-order ambiguities.

Among the hazard categories, the "struck-by" scenario achieved particularly strong results, both in CLIP alignment and object detection accuracy. This may be attributed to its relatively narrow thematic scope, often centered on excavator interactions, and the inclusion of clear, consistent visual patterns during LoRA fine-tuning. By contrast, the "falls" and "caught-in" categories involved more diverse spatial configurations and object interactions, making them harder to model precisely. This suggests that further gains in quality may be achievable by refining the visual scope through domain-specific subclassification of hazard scenarios, enabling more targeted exemplar selection for LoRA fine-tuning. Crucially, these gains were achieved with a parameter-efficient setup. LoRA modules fine-tuned on 20 images per hazard and 30 background images substantially adapted the Base Model (Flux.1-dev). A sensitivity analysis that varied background configuration and per-class sample size indicates a practical operating point of at least 20 images per hazard class together with a background model trained on images from multiple sites. Under this setting, CLIP increased and FID decreased relative to 10-image sets, and the combined background yielded lower average FID than per-site models (Tables 5, 8). This efficiency is important in construction safety, where annotated data are scarce due to risk, privacy, and the infrequency of hazardous events. The lightweight nature of LoRA also supports scalability, since new modules can be trained independently to extend coverage to additional hazards or environmental styles with minimal overhead. The successful training of a YOLOv8 detector using only synthetic data, achieving an mAP@0.5:0.95 of 0.941 on real-world CCTV frames, provides strong evidence that the generated images contain sufficient visual cues for practical deployment. Performance remained high across all target classes, especially for "excavator" and "steel beam," including visually challenging conditions such as substructure zones with cluttered backgrounds and partial occlusion. The synthetic-only model even outperformed a baseline trained on real annotated data, underscoring the viability of synthetic datasets as substitutes for manual annotation in safety-critical detection pipelines.

Several factors may underlie this superior performance. First, the synthetic dataset was intentionally designed to highlight safety-critical object configurations such as worker proximity to moving equipment or entrapment scenarios, which may provide stronger semantic cues than passively collected real-world annotations. Second, class distributions were carefully balanced during generation to ensure sufficient training signals across all hazard types, in contrast to real-world data which often exhibits skewed or sparse representation of critical events. Third, the use of prompt-defined labels combined with manual quality filtering minimized annotation inconsistencies commonly observed in manually labeled datasets, thereby enhancing training stability. Finally, the prompt-based generation process allowed for the deliberate emphasis of hazard-relevant visual elements (e.g., occluded workers, equipment motion), enhancing the model's ability to recognize subtle patterns. These aspects likely contributed to the improved detection performance observed in the synthetic-only model, particularly for rare or visually complex safety scenarios. This advantage is reflected in the class-wise mAP@0.5:0.95 gains reported in Table 10, where the synthetic model achieved higher accuracy than the real-data baseline across all target classes. To provide a deeper interpretation of this performance gap and address potential concerns regarding background overfitting, the nature of the training data and the model's robustness were critically analyzed. Regarding the baseline, although the real-world dataset was expertly annotated, it inherently reflects a 'passive' monitoring state where safety-critical instances are sparse. In contrast, the synthetic generation functions as an 'active' sampling strategy, providing a high density of hazard-specific supervision that allows the model to learn semantic features more efficiently. Furthermore, the possibility that the synthetic model merely memorized background textures (i.e., overfitting to the Background LoRA) is contradicted by the wide-FOV stress test results. Although the performance on this unseen viewpoint set decreased to 0.858 (mAP@0.5:0.95) due to reduced object resolution, it notably remained comparable to the model trained on real data (0.852). If the model had overfitted to the specific background patterns of the training set, a catastrophic performance drop would have been expected under such geometric shifts. This resilience confirms that the Multi-LoRA approach successfully guided the model to learn robust, object-centric features rather than relying on background artifacts.

Despite these promising outcomes, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the limited diversity of the fine-tuning dataset may constrain generalization to unseen environments or rare hazard configurations. Second, although qualitative filtering was essential for ensuring data quality, the reliance on manual curation introduces subjectivity. Third, the current framework primarily reflects fixed-view surveillance settings commonly found on construction sites. While this aligns with the characteristics of the curated CCTV dataset, it may limit applicability in broader monitoring scenarios involving dynamic viewpoints, such as those from PTZ cameras or UAVs. Expanding the framework to accommodate diverse fields of view represents an important direction for future development. Furthermore, while CLIP and FID serve as useful quantitative proxies for assessing semantic alignment and visual realism, they may fall short in capturing safety-critical attributes such as occlusion plausibility, depth perception, material textures, or hazard severity, factors that are often essential for downstream tasks. These metrics served primarily to validate the internal consistency of the generated images and to empirically support the utility of the proposed Multi-LoRA architecture. Nevertheless, they remain limited in their ability to capture domain-specific realism essential for safety applications.

To meaningfully evaluate synthetic data for safety monitoring beyond these proxies, more domain-aware assessment protocols are needed, particularly those that reflect the full pipeline of visual hazard recognition in construction. Beyond object detection, future work should aim to establish comprehensive evaluation frameworks that span from safety scenario generation to model-based hazard interpretation. For example, accident case databases can be used to construct realistic text prompts that reflect plausible safety incidents. These prompts can then be paired with generated images to fine-tune a Vision-Language Model (VLM) for safety-specific captioning or risk assessment. The resulting outputs can be evaluated against ground-truth annotations provided by domain experts, enabling both qualitative and quantitative assessment of model performance in interpreting complex construction hazards. Such a pipeline, encompassing scenario definition, image synthesis, VLM fine-tuning, and expert-grounded validation, would offer a more rigorous path to verifying whether synthetic data supports reliable safety monitoring. Lastly, this study focused on only three hazard types; scaling to more diverse or composite scenarios remains a challenge for future expansion. This study lays the groundwork for such a direction by proposing a domain-adaptive image generation method that balances visual realism with targeted semantic control under data-scarce conditions. While current evaluations focus on general visual metrics and downstream object detection performance, the long-term goal is to extend this framework toward comprehensive safety-aware model development and validation.

Future work should complement internal validation by benchmarking recent synthetic data generation frameworks, including game-engine approaches (e.g., Unity or Unreal) and diffusion-based methods such as ODGEN. These methods often emphasize photorealism or object-centric rendering, providing strong baselines for detection performance in controlled settings. While this study prioritized semantic controllability and the faithful representation of construction-specific contexts over visual fidelity alone, comparative evaluation could elucidate the trade-offs between contextual realism and detection accuracy. Such benchmarking would further clarify the applicability and strengths of the proposed approach in safety-critical environments, where interpretability and hazard-specific nuance may be as important as raw detection metrics. In parallel, our evaluation of paraphrased prompts (P1–P3) in Table 3 and Fig. 7 demonstrated that the pipeline is relatively robust to common variations in text formulation. Nonetheless, broader prompt modalities, such as abstract descriptions, procedural narratives, or multimodal inputs, remain to be systematically explored to further assess semantic coverage. In addition, testing alternative encoders (e.g., BLIP-2 or domain-adapted text-image models) represents an important future direction for improving semantic alignment and robustness in hazard depiction across diverse construction contexts. Nevertheless, the ability of the proposed framework to train high-performing object detectors using only synthetic data without relying on real-world annotations demonstrates its strong potential. By enabling scalable and targeted data generation under resource constraints, this approach offers a viable alternative for vision-based safety monitoring in domains where real data is difficult, risky, or costly to obtain.

## 6. Conclusion

This paper addressed the persistent challenge of data scarcity in construction safety monitoring by proposing a lightweight domain adaptation framework. Leveraging the parameter-efficient design of LoRA, this research fine-tuned a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model (Flux.1-dev) on a small dataset comprising only 20 hazard images per category and 30 construction background scenes. Unlike prior approaches that rely on general-purpose generative models trained on large-scale datasets (e.g., LAION), our method employs a domain-specific fine-tuning strategy tailored to the distinct visual and semantic characteristics of construction environments. The core contribution of this work lies in its modular adaptation framework, which utilizes simultaneous Multi-LoRA blending during inference to combine independently trained background and hazard-specific features. This decomposition enables high-fidelity representation of both environmental context (e.g., camera perspectives, lighting conditions, site clutter) and safety-critical semantics (e.g., falls, struck-by incidents, caught-in hazards). This modularity not only enhances visual realism and semantic alignment but also facilitates scalable extension to new hazard types or site conditions, a critical requirement in the highly variable construction domain.

Quantitative results confirm the effectiveness of the approach: the Combined LoRAs configuration yielded the highest semantic alignment (as measured by CLIP scores), the lowest FID relative to real-world CCTV imagery, and strong object detection performance. A YOLOv8 model trained exclusively on the curated synthetic dataset achieved a mAP@0.5:0.95 of 94.1%, outperforming a baseline model trained on manually annotated real data. These findings demonstrate that LoRA-guided diffusion models can serve as practical substitutes for real data in safety-critical vision tasks, particularly in contexts where ethical, legal, or logistical barriers restrict data collection. In addition to its strong empirical performance, the proposed framework offers several practical benefits. It enables efficient domain adaptation with minimal data and computational overhead, supports modular reusability across diverse hazard scenarios, and offers fine-grained controllability through a node-based workflow that enables prompt-level customization and weighted Multi-LoRA blending. These characteristics make the proposed approach highly adaptable to the dynamic and unstructured nature of construction environments.

With future deployment in mind, broader integration of this framework into real-world safety systems offers significant potential for practical impact. Its ability to generate high-quality training data with minimal input makes it well-suited for rapid deployment in resource-constrained environments or emerging construction sites where labeled data are unavailable. By enabling cost-effective scaling and adaptability to site-specific conditions, the proposed approach bridges a critical gap between advanced generative AI techniques and the practical needs of construction safety management. As AI-driven oversight becomes increasingly integrated into construction workflows, methods such as this, grounded in semantic precision and operational flexibility, will be essential for ensuring both worker safety and technological accountability.

Future research can build on this work in several promising directions. Expanding the hazard taxonomy to encompass a broader and more fine-grained range of construction safety scenarios, such as task-specific risks in formwork dismantling or material transport through narrow zones, could help capture subtler factors beyond the primary accident types. This study focused on three of the four leading causes of construction fatalities identified by OSHA: falls, struck-by, and caught-in. Incorporating less visually discernible hazards such as electrocution remains an important avenue for future research. Compound scenarios, including simultaneous hazards and multi-worker interactions in confined spaces, also present important test cases for evaluating the framework's scalability and robustness. In addition, certain hazards, such as moving-object collisions or fall risks associated with behavioral patterns near floor openings, may only become apparent when temporal dynamics are considered. This underscores the need to explore video-based modeling approaches that can capture both spatial and temporal patterns of risk. Structured analyses of real-world incident reports and expert-guided scenario modeling may further improve the generation of synthetic data to more accurately reflect the complexity of construction site hazards. Diversifying hazard representation based on construction phases, work types, and underlying causes would enhance the generalizability and real-world applicability of the proposed framework. Moreover, future studies may examine cross-site validation to assess the generalizability of the synthetic training approach across diverse construction environments. Complementary to this, ablation studies could rigorously assess the specific contribution of the proposed Multi-LoRA architecture. Comparative evaluations against full-model fine-tuning and other parameter-efficient techniques, such as DreamBooth and ControlNet, would further clarify the practical advantages of the modular design under real-world constraints. Lastly, integrating temporal consistency for video-based applications and applying hybrid training strategies that combine synthetic and limited real-world data could improve robustness in continuous monitoring systems.
