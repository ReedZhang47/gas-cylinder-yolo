## Abstract

Data scarcity in construction hinders deep neural network training for computer vision applications. While synthetic data generators provide annotated images, they lack realism, creating a reality gap that leads to suboptimal real-world performance. This paper introduces BCon, a framework that integrates BlendCon, a construction data generation engine, with ControlNet, a generative architecture featuring conditioning controls, to enhance the realism and diversity of synthetic images while preserving annotations. Through hyperparameter tuning and post-processing, a dataset of 25,600 enhanced images is created. Quantitative evaluations demonstrate significant improvements in realism metrics: DreamSim (+9.5%), VIEScore (+114.3%), CLIPScore (+14.7%), and FID-5K (+22.6%), indicating closer alignment with real images. Moreover, YOLOv10 models trained on enhanced images achieve an AP50–95 of 0.66 on worker detection, outperforming those trained on original synthetic data by 7.9% and slightly surpassing models trained on equivalently sized real data. This framework offers cost-effective, high-quality dataset generation for visual AI applications in construction.

## 1. Introduction

While recent advancements in deep learning offer great promise for construction-related computer vision tasks (e.g., hazard detection or progress monitoring), their widespread adoption is hindered by their suboptimal real-world performance, which is mainly due to the lack of adequate, high-quality, construction-specific training data [1–3]. A review of the literature reveals merely two publicly available construction datasets: MOCS [4], containing 40,000 worker instances, and SODA [5], with 30,000 worker instances.

Best-performing object detectors are fully supervised, data-hungry deep neural networks (DNNs) that require far more annotated images. For instance, Tesla's Autopilot system was trained on six billion real-world object labels [6]. Acquiring such copious amounts of data in construction is nearly impossible due to time, cost, safety, and privacy concerns, not to mention that manual annotation remains a highly labor-intensive process [7,8]. As an illustration, annotating an image with 2D bounding boxes, segmentation masks, and classifications costs 0.97 USD, while generating 3D bounding boxes, 3D human poses, and depth maps is even more challenging, as it requires specialized sensors [9].

Two solutions have emerged to address data scarcity: synthetic data generation via graphical simulation engines and generative AI models. This study summarizes the limitations and potential of each and proposes an advanced method that combines their strengths.

Graphical simulation engines, such as Blender [10], are software platforms that render synthetic scenes based on 3D models, lighting, textures, and camera configurations. Data generation via these engines is cheap, fast, and comes with full annotations, making it suitable for training multimodal DNNs [11,12]. Tools like BlendCon [13] generate fully annotated synthetic construction site images with controllable image configurations while eliminating real-world data collection concerns. However, most graphically generated synthetic data suffers from the reality gap, referring to the discrepancy between synthetic and real images [14]. Consequently, models trained solely on synthetic data with a large reality gap perform poorly in real-world applications, as such data augmentation does not translate to improved model performance [15,16]. This gap is pronounced in complex construction environments, where visual realism is critical for accurate object detection, as it allows models to effectively recognize varied lighting, textures, and details [17].

Generative AI models, by contrast, have achieved remarkable success in producing high-quality photorealistic images at low cost, but unlike graphical simulators, they lack essential annotations for supervised learning tasks. Manual annotation of the generated images is prohibitively time-consuming and expensive, negating their potential for data augmentation despite their superior visual realism.

In summary, graphical simulators provide annotated data but suffer from the reality gap, while generative AI produces photorealistic images without essential annotations, making both approaches individually insufficient. This paper introduces BCon, a fully automated, computational framework that bridges this gap by uniting both approaches and combining their strengths. BCon integrates a fully controllable Blender-based simulation engine with a diffusion-based generative refinement stage, yielding fully annotated, realistic construction-site synthetic images.

BCon's effectiveness is demonstrated through improvements in multiple realism evaluation metrics and enhanced object (worker) detection performance. By eliminating the need for costly and time-consuming manual annotation, BCon offers a scalable, practical, and privacy-preserving data generation solution accessible to both researchers and industry practitioners. This advancement could pave the way for broader adoption of deep learning models in construction applications, where high-quality annotated data has long been a limiting factor.

## 2. Research objectives and contributions

This study aims to develop and validate a fully automated synthetic data generation framework that combines graphical simulation with generative AI to produce realistic, yet fully annotated synthetic images for training DNNs for construction applications. By incorporating the generative architecture, ControlNet, into the established BlendCon framework, the present study introduces BCon, a framework that unifies the strengths of both approaches. The simulation component of BlendCon generates fully annotated synthetic images with precise control over scene configurations. Meanwhile, the generative AI component enhances the visual realism and diversity of these images, particularly the backgrounds, without altering the spatial alignment of annotations. Here, achieving diversity refers to producing a wide range of unique images that capture real-world variability, thereby improving the scalability of models [18]. The research is therefore centered around the following core objectives (See Fig. 1):

- To enhance the photorealism of synthetic construction-site images generated by BlendCon while preserving ground-truth annotations, the framework integrates a ControlNet-guided generative refinement stage. To optimize ControlNet's performance specifically for this application, a systematic hyperparameter tuning process is conducted, guided by GPT-4o-based perceptual scoring.

- To measure realism gains objectively, a complementary suite of automated perceptual metrics is selected and applied, enabling bias-free quantitative evaluation.

- To verify the downstream utility of the enhanced data, deep neural networks are trained on three datasets—real, baseline synthetic, and enhanced synthetic—and their AP0.5–0.95 performance on worker detection is compared.

This research makes several key contributions to the field of synthetic data generation and domain adaptation in construction studies. First, it introduces integration of generative AI with a Blender-based simulation engine to bridge the persistent reality gap in synthetic data. By combining the controllability of simulation with generative AI's ability to enhance realism, the resulting framework serves as a systematized solution for generating realistic, yet fully annotated images that future construction researchers can benchmark and build upon. Second, an extensive, automated tuning process is conducted for ControlNet hyperparameters, yielding a high-performing configuration that can serve as a ready-made starting point, saving subsequent researchers considerable effort when adopting ControlNet to their own problems. Lastly, the approach is rigorously validated using both quantitative realism metrics and downstream object detection performance, providing robust evidence of its effectiveness in bridging the sim-to-real domain gap. It confirms that users can generate low-cost, privacy-preserving, and high-quality datasets for their specific applications.

From an industrial perspective, the approach offers an affordable synthetic data generation solution that is accessible even to non-experts. It equips construction start-ups, robotics companies, and other industry practitioners with the resources needed to develop DNN-based solutions for key applications, such as hazard detection, progress tracking, and quality monitoring, facilitating the integration of AI-driven techniques, automation, and digitization in construction.

## 3. Related work

Graphical simulation engines have shown promise in addressing the scarcity of high-quality annotated data for construction vision tasks by enabling controllable scene generation with automatic labels [13]. However, the utility of such synthetic data is often limited by its lack of realism [16]. While some domain adaptation techniques have improved transferability, they face inherent limitations [19]. Conversely, generative AI models produce highly realistic images but lack annotations. Fusing these complementary approaches has potential to overcome their individual shortcomings. This section reviews prior work across these domains and highlights how recent advances in conditional generative modeling offer a promising avenue for closing the realism gap in simulated data while preserving access to precise, automatically generated labels.

### 3.1. Synthetic data generation in the construction domain

The construction industry has increasingly turned to synthetic data generation to address challenges associated with data scarcity [16]. Earlier works [21,22] utilized virtual data generated by building information models for visual recognition tasks in construction. For example, Hong et al. [21] proposed an image-to-image translation pipeline that converts BIM-generated renderings into photorealistic scene images for building and bridge detection. However, BIM models are engineered for design precision and clarity, often omitting real-world imperfections and resulting in overly idealized scenes that lack the visual clutter and disorder inherent to actual construction environments. More importantly, these studies focused exclusively on static structural components, minimizing the focus on the synthesis of mobile entities like human workers due to modeling limitations. While some researchers, such as Neuhausen et al. [23], have explored the inclusion of human workers, these efforts were confined to scenarios with limited diversity in background textures, worker motions, avatar designs, and imaging conditions, all failing to represent realistic settings. In an effort to address these limitations by enhancing background diversity and realism, Kim et al. [20] have employed the SURREAL framework to superimpose 3D synthetic construction-worker avatars onto real 2D backgrounds to augment training data, but this approach introduces spatial inconsistencies, such as mismatches in scale, lighting, and perspective, that compromises overall image realism. Recognizing these limitations, recent advancements like BlendCon have focused on providing greater control in synthesizing worker-centric construction data, addressing many of the challenges faced by earlier approaches [13] (see Fig. 2).

*Fig. 2. Examples of synthetic construction images.*
- Note: The top row shows images from the prior study [20], while the bottom row presents images generated by BlendCon [13].

BlendCon generates annotated synthetic construction site images by arranging 3D human avatars, equipment, and environments, reflecting real-world variability in layouts and object placements. The framework produces comprehensive annotations alongside synthetic images, including 2D/3D bounding boxes and dense poses, semantic segmentation masks, and a per-pixel depth map. BlendCon demonstrates effectiveness with YOLOv7 models, achieving an AP0.5–0.95 (Average Precision calculated at different Intersection over Union thresholds) of 71% in worker detection tasks, closely matching the 75% performance of models trained on real data, and even outperforming them for Intersection over Union (IoU) thresholds above 85% [13].

While synthetic data can be effective, a growing body of research has consistently shown that simply increasing the volume of low-quality data does not guarantee improved model performance. In fact, smaller but higher-quality datasets that are well-aligned with the target domain often surpass larger, noisier counterparts with significant domain gaps [24–27]. These findings underscore the importance of quality over sheer quantity in synthetic data generation, suggesting that enhancing visual realism and domain alignment should take precedence over indiscriminate scaling. Improving data quality reduces the domain gap, enabling models trained on synthetic data to achieve performance comparable to or even surpassing those trained exclusively on real-world data [14].

### 3.2. Domain adaptation: opportunities and challenges

While graphical simulation engines offer the potential for greater realism, simulating every detail (e.g., micro-surface roughness and material wear on concrete or steel) at such a level of precision may not be a practical use of computational resources. A more cost-effective alternative to painstakingly simulating photorealistic details is domain adaptation, which transfers knowledge from a source domain (synthetic data) to a target domain (real data) [28]. By aligning the feature distributions of synthetic and real images, domain adaptation techniques enable models trained on synthetic data to perform effectively on real-world tasks [19]. Existing methods fall into several broad categories: domain invariant feature learning [29,30], pseudo-label-based self-training [31,32], domain randomization [33,34], mean-teacher training [35–37], and image-to-image translation [38].

Among various strategies, image-to-image translation has garnered particular attention as it directly addresses the domain gap at the input level by transforming images to visually resemble the target domain, leading to better performance compared to techniques that only adjust feature representations [28]. Within this paradigm, a number of methods have been developed to enhance the synthetic images, primarily relying on adversarial training through Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) [39–42]. Several efforts have also applied GAN-based image translation within the construction domain, extending beyond human detection to broader construction objects and scenes. For instance, Pham and Han [43] have developed a GAN-based model to generate realistic excavator images from synthetic inputs for the purpose of 3D pose estimation. Similarly, Bresson et al. [44] have designed a GAN architecture capable of translating seven-channel, 360-degree synthetic urban scenes into photorealistic images of Paris streets [44]. Despite these advances, a persistent limitation remains: As with many GAN-based approaches, even their most visually convincing outputs reveal significant perceptible shortcomings in visual quality and scene coherence. This raises important questions about whether such translations can truly serve as viable substitutes or complements to real-world data.

Furthermore, GAN-based domain adaptation methods encounter significant limitations beyond visual quality, including complex training procedures and, more importantly, a lack of user control over the stylization process. Their unconstrained generators often distort object geometry and scene structure, leading to annotation misalignment and compromised spatial fidelity [45]. Since users have no control over the black-box models, these conventional approaches alter the spatial structure of source images during transformation, resulting in unrealistic output images and a loss of annotation accuracy [28].

### 3.3. State-of-the-art high-fidelity generative models for domain adaptation

Recent generative models, such as DALL-E 3 [46], Imagen 3 [47], and Stable Diffusion XL [48], can create realistic images with fine-grained details and diverse styles, operating in zero-shot conditions without additional training or fine-tuning. For instance, DATID-3D [49] leverages text-to-image diffusion models to generate photorealistic images of 3D objects, but it does not provide any annotations, limiting its utility for supervised downstream tasks [49]. As another example, a forthcoming publication employs the generative model Midjourney to synthesize hyperrealistic construction site images [50]. A worker detection model trained on 33,700 of these synthetic instances achieved an AP50–95 of 64.2%, approaching the 70.5% achieved by a model trained on an equivalent number of real instances from the SODA and MOCS datasets. Furthermore, augmenting the real dataset with the synthetic images boosted performance to an AP50–95 of 79.9% [50]. Despite all these strengths, similar to other diffusion-based approaches, the absence of accompanying annotations posed a major limitation, as manually producing simple 2D bounding box labels for all generated images required significant time and cost.

Generative models have emerged as a promising tool for domain adaptation by producing highly realistic images that better resemble the target domain. However, since they do not inherently preserve spatial structures in the original inputs, the transformations they apply can misalign with existing annotations. This misalignment is a critical limitation, as even slight annotation inaccuracy can significantly degrade model performance. Experiments have shown that minor perturbations in bounding box annotations can lead to a drop of over 8% in average precision [51,52].

The integration of advanced generative models, such as diffusion models, with graphical data simulation engines like BlendCon has the potential to establish a framework capable of producing synthetic images that are both photorealistic and fully annotated, thus leveraging the complementary strengths of the two paradigms. This combined approach would have the potential to bridge the reality gap more effectively than existing domain adaptation methods. Unlike pure simulation approaches that suffer from the reality gap, this pipeline delivers photorealism, and unlike standalone generative AI models, whose outputs lack annotations, it preserves all ground-truth labels, eliminating the need for manual relabeling.

### 3.4. ControlNet: A conditional diffusion-based generative architecture

The shortcomings of unconditional diffusion and GAN-based translators point to a simple remedy: constraining the generator with explicit guidance from edge maps, depth maps, or segmentation masks. Conditional generative models offer this escape hatch. These models range from traditional conditional GANs (cGANs) [53] to advanced diffusion-based models with conditioning mechanisms, such as T2I-adapter [54] and ControlNet [55]. Traditional cGANs exhibit several critical shortcomings. First, they generally require large, carefully paired datasets to learn reliable mappings, which are seldom available in specialized domains like construction [56]. Second, adversarial training is notoriously unstable and prone to mode collapse, often producing artifacts or repeating scene elements [57,58]. Third, cGANs lack explicit structural constraints and frequently warp object geometry and misalign annotations during translation, as vanilla cGANs were not designed with spatial control in mind [59]. Even when spatial conditions are used, the model may struggle to maintain geometric accuracy, especially when dealing with complex, high-resolution scenes [58,60].

ControlNet is explicitly designed to overcome these shortcomings by incorporating explicit spatial conditions into a diffusion-based backbone. To elaborate, by integrating conditional inputs, ControlNet provides fine-grained control over the generative process, effectively preserving the spatial structure of source images while allowing controlled perturbations [55]. It has been widely applied in image editing to modify specific aspects of an image while keeping others unchanged [61–63]. However, its potential as a means of domain adaptation is not fully realized, and to the best of the authors' knowledge, this study is the first to explore its application for enhancing synthetic construction images.

While ControlNet shows promising capabilities in enhancing image realism, its effective application is not straightforward. The performance of ControlNet is highly sensitive to a range of hyperparameters, such as number of inference steps, conditioning scales, and classifier-free guidance scale, which significantly influence the quality of the generated images. Variations in these hyperparameters can lead to substantial differences in output image realism and overall visual appeal. Consequently, adopting this technology requires careful hyperparameter tuning, through systematic experimentation and objective realism assessment. This study demonstrates the critical impact of these hyperparameters and presents a strategy for selecting optimal configurations tailored to the downstream task of enhancing synthetic construction imagery.

## 4. BCon: BlendCon ControlNet

Fig. 3 presents an overview of the proposed image realism enhancement framework, BCon, which sequentially integrates BlendCon and ControlNet. This sequential process begins with the generation of fully annotated synthetic images using BlendCon (Fig. 3(a)). BlendCon accepts as input a randomly posed animated worker avatar, a processed 3D construction scene, a randomly selected scene horizon, and configurable lighting and camera settings. The lighting settings include source strength, angle, and rotation, while the camera parameters cover focal length, depth of field, and sensor size. These inputs are used within Blender to render fully annotated synthetic construction site images. Its outputs include RGB synthetic images along with their full annotations, namely, 2D/3D bounding boxes and key points for human avatars, semantic segmentation masks, and per-pixel depth maps for the entire image (Fig. 3(a)).

The enhancement stage then begins by feeding each synthetic RGB image, together with its corresponding depth map and semantic mask, into the ControlNet architecture as the main input and guiding conditions, respectively (Fig. 3(b)). Additionally, positive (descriptive) and negative (restrictive) textual prompts are employed to steer the diffusion process toward desired visual characteristics. Through iterative denoising steps, the pretrained Stable Diffusion backbone within ControlNet refines the input images, enhancing the overall visual realism while strictly preserving the original spatial structure and annotations. The result is a set of high-quality, realism-enhanced RGB images that maintain perfect alignment with the initial labels.

*Fig. 3. Overview of the proposed image realism enhancement framework, BCon.*

ControlNet offers extensive user control through its numerous hyperparameters, such as conditioning scale and number of inference steps, which, if not carefully selected, can adversely affect the quality of the generated images. To optimize ControlNet's performance, a comprehensive hyperparameter tuning phase is conducted prior to full-dataset enhancement. This prerequisite step systematically explored ControlNet's extensive hyperparameter space to pinpoint the settings that deliver the highest visual quality; the resulting configuration is subsequently applied across the synthetic corpus. The significance of this tuning stage and its impact on image realism is examined in detail in the ablation study (see Section 5.4).

Finally, to ensure anatomical accuracy and maintain the integrity of key point annotations of construction workers, an optional "avatar cut and paste" procedure is introduced (Fig. 3(c)). This involves extracting worker avatars from the initial BlendCon-generated images using their corresponding semantic masks and superimposing them onto their ControlNet-enhanced counterparts. This step helps preserve precise human anatomies and key point annotations, though it is demonstrated later that it is not essential for achieving realism improvements or enhancing DNN performance and can be omitted without adverse effects (see Section 5.4).

Fig. 4 presents example images from the original BlendCon and the BCon-enhanced images. The BlendCon images in row (a) display samples of the original synthetic scenes prior to enhancement, exhibiting simplistic textures, uniform lighting, and a lack of environmental complexity. Furthermore, the images within each scenario are highly similar to one another, as only the camera or the human figures move slightly between images, reflecting limited diversity in visual appearance. In contrast, the BCon-enhanced images in row (b) demonstrate remarkable improvements in texture detail, realistic lighting, and scene diversity. The surfaces display more natural variations, and the overall ambiance closely resembles that of real construction sites, providing a more diverse and realistic representation. This visual comparison demonstrates an enhanced level of realism. To systematically assess these perceived improvements, several widely adopted realism metrics are described in Section 4.4. A full quantitative evaluation using these metrics across original, enhanced, and real images is provided in Section 5.2. As an early indication, for the specific enhancement cases depicted in Fig. 4, the VIEScore [64] shows an approximate increase of 73.6%, suggesting a substantial improvement in visual realism even at a small scale.

### 4.1. Impact of stable diffusion version on image enhancement

BCon is designed to work with diffusion models, making it inherently adaptable; as newer, more capable diffusion models become available, they can be seamlessly integrated to further improve image enhancement performance. In initial attempts to enhance the BlendCon images, ControlNet was employed with Stable Diffusion v1.5 (SD-1.5). However, the results were suboptimal, producing unrealistic and distorted images, particularly in indoor environments. Recognizing these limitations, a transition was made to Stable Diffusion XL (SD-XL 1.0) [65] (developed by StabilityAI) and its associated variational autoencoder (SDXL-VAE) [66], leading to significant improvements in image quality and realism. Fig. 5 presents a comparison of images processed through ControlNet using SD-1.5 and SD-XL, alongside the original BlendCon images. In row (a), the original BlendCon images display the baseline synthetic images. Images in row (b) show the result of applying ControlNet with SD-1.5, demonstrating minimal improvements and unrealistic textures and lighting. In contrast, row (c) presents the outcomes of using ControlNet with SD-XL, which exhibits enhancements in texture, lighting, and overall quality, closely resembling a real-world construction site. The superior performance of SD-XL is attributed to its larger capacity (2.6 billion parameters compared to 860 million in SD-1.5 [48]) and architectural advancements.

### 4.2. Hyperparameter tuning

To systematically identify hyperparameters that consistently affect image realism across different configurations, a series of experiments is conducted, enabling their classification into independent and interdependent groups. In short, the independent hyperparameters were fixed at their optimal values, and interdependent ones were subjected to a comprehensive grid search, during which an automated realism evaluation using GPT-4o, validated by human experts, was employed to identify the optimal hyperparameter combinations. The following subsections detail the method and results of this tuning. It is important to note that this work does not propose a universal or generalizable strategy for hyperparameter tuning. Instead, a specific approach based on classical grid search is described to systematically explore parameter space in this scenario. The motivation for this approach, and the rationale for not relying on commonly used random values, are further discussed in the ablation study (Section 5.4).

#### 4.2.1. Identifying and classifying ControlNet hyperparameters

Table 1 lists eight ControlNet hyperparameters that significantly influence output quality. By carefully tuning these hyperparameters, the visual realism of the output images can be maximized while ensuring that the original structural information remains accurate.

*Table 1. Overview of eight critical hyperparameters of ControlNet.*

| Hyperparameter | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Inference steps | Number of denoising iterations in the diffusion process, directly affecting image quality and processing time. |
| Conditioning scales | Weights assigned to conditioning inputs (e.g., depth maps, semantic masks) that modulate the influence of spatial cues. |
| Image strength | Degree to which the input image influences the final output, balancing preservation of original content with new details. |
| Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) scale | Balances adherence to the provided textual prompts. |
| Output image size | Determines the resolution of the generated images, with higher sizes requiring more computational resources. |
| Solver choice | Selection of the noise scheduler during diffusion, impacting convergence speed and output image quality. |
| Positive text prompt | Provides explicit guidance on the desired visual characteristics of the output images. |
| Negative text prompt | Specifies constraints to suppress undesirable features. |

To manage the complexity of the tuning process, a range of plausible values is first defined for each hyperparameter (as illustrated in Table 2), and preliminary experiments are conducted to identify those that consistently influenced image realism regardless of the values of other hyperparameters and could therefore be fixed at optimal values. Such hyperparameters are classified as independent if their adjustment consistently improves image realism across three different random configurations of other hyperparameters. Conversely, if the effect of a hyperparameter on image realism varies depending on the values of other hyperparameters, meaning that its impact is not consistent and could either enhance or diminish realism depending on the context, it is considered an interdependent hyperparameter. For these interdependent hyperparameters, an optimal value cannot be determined in isolation. Therefore, a comprehensive grid search is implemented to systematically explore all possible combinations and identify the ideal set that maximizes image realism while preserving annotations.

To determine which hyperparameters are independent, experiments are conducted by varying one hyperparameter at a time across its specified values while assigning random values from their respective ranges in Table 2 to the remaining hyperparameters. For each value of the hyperparameter under investigation, three images are generated using three different random configurations for the other hyperparameters. The realism of these images is then visually assessed, focusing on whether changes in the hyperparameter consistently lead to improvements in image realism regardless of the settings of the other hyperparameters.

Through this process, it is identified that adjusting certain hyperparameters consistently enhances image realism. For instance, larger images consistently improve quality across all configurations, demonstrating independent influence, although this requires increased processing power and time. Similarly, the solver choice and the positive text prompt demonstrate consistent effects on image realism, irrespective of other hyperparameters. Although negative prompts are designed to exclude unwanted characteristics, experiments indicate that they have minimal influence on output quality compared to other hyperparameters. Accordingly, the negative prompt is kept consistent throughout the experiments, allowing tuning efforts to concentrate on more impactful parameters. Output image size, solver choice, and both positive and negative textual prompts are therefore classified as independent hyperparameters. To streamline the tuning process, these independent hyperparameters are fixed at their optimal values, as detailed in Table 3.

*Table 3. Optimal values for independent hyperparameters.*

| Hyperparameter | Selected value |
| --- | --- |
| Output image size | 1280 × 1280 |
| Solver choice | DDIMScheduler |
| Positive text prompt | "A high-quality, high-resolution image of a construction site" |
| Negative text prompt | "Blurry, blurred, bad anatomy, low quality" |

The remaining four hyperparameters, namely the number of inference steps, conditioning scales, image strength, and CFG scale, do not show consistent effects on image realism when varied individually, and their impact on image quality depended on the values of the other hyperparameters. For example, increasing the CFG scale did not always improve image realism; its effect varied based on the settings of the conditioning scales and image strength.

#### 4.2.2. Grid search for interdependent hyperparameters and automated evaluation using GPT-4o

A comprehensive grid search is performed on four interdependent hyperparameters: (1) inference steps, (2) conditioning scales, (3) image strength, and (4) CFG scale, with the goal of identifying the optimal combination that maximizes image realism while maintaining annotation integrity. This results in a total of 4 × 4 × 3 × 4 = 192 unique hyperparameter combinations. To ensure a diverse and representative evaluation, a sample of 60 images from 20 different BlendCon scenarios is selected, covering a broad spectrum of content and complexity. Images within each BlendCon scenario are similar but exhibit slight differences due to worker or camera movements, providing variability within the dataset. Each hyperparameter combination is applied to these images, resulting in a total of 11,520 enhanced images (192 combinations × 60 images). While this extensive dataset allows us to comprehensively assess the effects of each hyperparameter setting on image realism and annotation preservation, evaluating the realism of such a large number of images visually is impractical and subjective. Therefore, an automated and objective method is necessary to efficiently evaluate image realism at scale.

Recent studies have explored using large language models with image understanding capabilities, such as GPT-4o [67], to evaluate image realism systematically, reporting that these models can process visual information and generate assessments that correlate well with human judgments [68,69]. Building on these findings, GPT-4o is employed to automate the realism assessment by instructing it to rate each generated image on a scale from 1 to 10 based on perceived realism. The model is provided with the following prompt along with the image:

"Rate the realism of the attached synthetic construction site image from 1 to 10 (decimals allowed). Provide only a single numerical score as your response, with no explanations. A rating of 1 represents highly unrealistic images, exhibiting issues such as incorrect shadows, lighting, texture inconsistencies, or perspective errors. A score of 10 signifies photorealistic images."

### 4.4. Realism evaluation metrics

Realism is inherently subjective; therefore, objective assessment requires computational metrics that correlate with human perception. Among traditional approaches, the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) [70] has been one of the most widely used metrics for evaluating the quality and realism of generated images. In the case of comparing real and synthetic datasets, FID operates by first extracting high-level semantic features from both real and synthetic images using a pre-trained network. These features are treated as samples drawn from multivariate Gaussian distributions, one for each set (real and generated). FID then measures the distance between these two distributions by comparing their means and covariances. Mathematically, this involves computing how far apart the centers of the distributions are (mean difference) and how different their spreads are (covariance difference). A lower FID score indicates that the generated images are more similar to real images in terms of feature distribution, thus suggesting higher visual realism [70].

In recent years, several alternative metrics, such as DreamSim [71], VIEScore [64], and CLIPScore [72] have been introduced and shown to align more closely with human judgments of visual realism in comparison to FID [64,71–73]. These newer metrics have gained broad acceptance in the generative AI community and are increasingly adopted in recent literature for objective realism evaluation. Following this trend, these metrics are utilized in this study to quantitatively measure realism improvements, complemented by manual visual inspections to qualitatively validate the results.

To elaborate, DreamSim is a perceptual similarity metric designed to capture human-aligned visual realism across multiple semantic and structural dimensions, including object layout, pose, and color consistency [71]. Unlike traditional distribution-based metrics such as FID, which primarily assess the distance between high-level feature distributions, DreamSim is trained directly on a dataset of human similarity judgments using diffusion-generated images [71]. In practice, it embeds two images into a learned feature space by combining outputs from multiple pre-trained vision backbones (e.g., CLIP [74]), and computes the distance between these embeddings to reflect perceptual dissimilarity from a given reference image [71]. When applied to the synthetic and real image pairs, DreamSim measures how closely the images in the BlendCon and BCon datasets resemble real images in terms of human-perceived visual coherence, offering a more nuanced assessment of realism that captures mid-level features often overlooked by traditional statistical metrics.

Complementing this, VIEScore is another visual evaluation metric that leverages large multimodal language models like GPT to evaluate images based on semantic content and visual quality, providing separate scores for semantic consistency (how well the image matches intended content or conditions) and perceptual quality (visual aesthetics, artifact detection, realism) [64]. Unlike single-score metrics, VIEScore integrates both conditions (e.g., background, object placement) and the generated image into a single query processed by a multimodal LLM. The model then outputs a numerical score for each dimension, along with natural-language rationales, reflecting human-like reasoning. Its performance is reported to align closely with human judgments.

Moreover, CLIPScore metric measures the semantic alignment (cosine similarity) between an image and a textual description using Contrastive Language-Image Pretraining (CLIP) [74], where a higher similarity indicates stronger semantic alignment, achieving the highest reported correlation with human assessments [72]. In addition to measuring alignment, CLIPScore has been widely used to measure reasoning, knowledge, robustness, and fairness of generative models [73]. In this case, contextual alignment understanding capability is relied upon to measure the consistency between BCon images and a textual prompt similar to the one used during their generation, reflecting how closely they match intended realistic visual content. Since this approach measures contextual realism relative to a consistent target prompt [73], the same evaluation strategy is applied to assess the semantic alignment of BlendCon and real images as a means of comparative analysis, even though they were not generated with any specific textual description. Comparing CLIPScores across BCon, BlendCon, and real images measures the alignment of synthetic and real images with domain-relevant textual context.

## 5. Improved realism and YOLOv10 performance: Experiments and results

This section evaluates BCon end-to-end, quantifying its effect on both image realism and downstream worker-detection performance. The datasets and splits used are first described (Section 5.1), followed by reporting of realism gains using DreamSim, VIEScore, CLIPScore, and FID, alongside a feature-space analysis via YOLOv10 backbone embeddings and t-SNE (Section 5.2). Next, YOLOv10-M is trained on three datasets: original BlendCon, BCon-enhanced, and real, while holding instance counts and training settings constant to enable fair comparison (Section 5.3). Finally, ablation studies isolate the contributions of hyperparameter tuning and the avatar cut-and-paste step (Section 5.4).

Similarly, the FID-5K score (computed using 5000 images from each dataset) decreases from 63.2 to 48.9, indicating a substantial reduction in the distributional gap between synthetic and real-world images. This lower FID confirms that the BCon-enhanced dataset more closely resembles real-world visuals in terms of their underlying feature distribution.

CLIPScores are computed for 10,000 images randomly selected from BlendCon, BCon, and real-world datasets using the prompt "A highly realistic image of a construction site." The BCon-enhanced images show a 14.7% improvement over the original BlendCon images, indicating better semantic alignment and enhanced realism. This suggests that the enhancements not only improved realism but also scene coherence and perceptual clarity. Scores are computed for a randomly selected set of 10,000 images from each dataset, with the overall VIEScore representing a weighted average of two components: semantic score and quality score. Notably, the BCon images demonstrate substantial improvements in both aspects, with the semantic score rising from 2.7 to 6.9 and the quality score increasing from 3.0 to 5.8, further highlighting the effectiveness of the enhancements.

To further investigate the feature representations learned from the different datasets, a feature extraction and visualization experiment is conducted. Specifically, 4256 images, comprising 1500 images from each of the BlendCon and BCon datasets and 1256 images from the scraped real-world dataset, are passed through the backbone of YOLOv10-M to extract high-dimensional feature vectors, as they capture essential features for object detection and provide meaningful representations of the images. To visualize and compare these feature representations, t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) [75] is employed to reduce the high-dimensional feature vectors to two dimensions while preserving their structural relationships.

The resulting t-SNE plot, illustrated in Fig. 7, reveals distinct patterns among the datasets. The features extracted from the BlendCon images form separate, highly dense clusters, indicating that these synthetic images have distinctive characteristics not aligned with real-world data. This clustering effect arises because BlendCon generates images within predefined scenarios where each image is highly similar to others in the same scenario, with only slight variations; thus, they form tight clusters in the feature space. In contrast, the features from BCon images lie much closer to the large, real-world cluster, reflecting a notable improvement in capturing variability and distribution when compared to BlendCon's compact, isolated clusters, which fail to represent the broader spread of real-world data. This proximity suggests that the enhancements made by the BCon framework enable the synthetic images to share more similar feature distributions with real images, effectively shifting the synthetic domain (source) toward the real domain (target).

### 5.3. Worker-detection DNN performance enhancement

Evaluating the performance of the resulting worker detection model is crucial for understanding the practical impact of the proposed contribution. To this end, the medium variant of YOLOv10 [76] is trained separately on three distinct datasets, original synthetic (BlendCon), enhanced synthetic (BCon), and available construction real datasets, and evaluates their AP50–95 performance on an unseen new real-world scraped test set. It should be noted that no hybrid datasets are created or used at this stage; that is, synthetic and real data are not combined, nor are original and enhanced synthetic images mixed. Each dataset was used purely and independently, enabling a fair and isolated comparison of how the enhancements alone contribute to downstream performance relative to their original and real-world counterparts.

All images are resized to 640 × 640 pixels to balance computational efficiency with detection performance. For each dataset, an 80/10/10 split for training, validation, and testing, respectively, is adopted. The models are trained for 800 epochs with the possibility of early stopping if the validation loss does not improve for 100 consecutive epochs. Notably, two models that initially halted between epochs 500 and 600 were further trained for additional epochs; however, their AP50–95 performance decreased by an average of 5.2%, underscoring the importance of early stopping in avoiding overfitting. To ensure a fair comparison of the effectiveness of the datasets, all models are trained using identical configurations. The results are summarized in Table 8.

*Table 8. YOLOv10-M performance across different datasets.*

| Dataset | # Instances | AP50–95 on scraped test set (%) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| BlendCon | 43,000 | 60.9 |
| BCon | 43,000 | 65.7 |
| Real: SODA+MOCS | 43,000 | 65.6 |

Table 8 highlights the substantial contribution of realism-oriented enhancement to downstream performance. Under identical training protocols and equal instance counts (43,000), the model trained on the BCon dataset achieves an AP50–95 of 65.7%, a 4.8% point gain over the baseline BlendCon model (60.9%). This margin confirms that the BCon framework not only mitigates the simulation-to-real domain gap but also translates its visual improvements into more discriminative feature learning. Remarkably, the BCon model marginally surpasses the real-data baseline (65.6%), demonstrating that carefully curated and enhanced synthetic imagery can result in equal, and even edge out, performance achieved with labor-intensive real annotations. Collectively, these findings validate BCon's effectiveness and underscore its potential to provide low-cost, scalable training data that rivals or exceeds real-world datasets in construction applications.

### 5.4. Ablation study

To isolate and evaluate the contributions of individual components within the BCon framework, a series of ablation studies is conducted, focusing on two key modules: (i) the hyperparameter tuning process, and (ii) the avatar cut-and-paste post-processing step. The goal is to quantify how each affects the downstream performance of an object detection model, specifically, the YOLOv10-M model, trained for worker detection.

#### 5.4.1. Hyperparameter tuning

In the first experiment, the importance of BCon's hyperparameter tuning stage is examined. To this end, 43,000 synthetic samples are enhanced using a randomly selected set of ControlNet hyperparameters from Table 2. The exact values used for this ablation are summarized in Table 9. Notably, while the hyperparameters used in this comparison are randomly selected from the predefined grid, the grid itself is composed of widely adopted and practically validated values commonly used in diffusion-based image generation pipelines. For instance, the resolution of 512 × 512 is the native training resolution of the original Stable Diffusion v1.5 model [77].

Specifically, the original resolution of the generated images (512 × 512) is retained without any upscaling. The resulting model achieves an AP50–95 of 21% on the same held-out scraped real test set, which is more than three times lower than the 65.7% AP50–95 obtained using the tuned hyperparameters. This dramatic drop in performance highlights the critical role of proper hyperparameter selection when using generative models for realism enhancement.

This result aligns with visual observations. As shown in Fig. 8, the images produced under this ablated setting are largely unstructured and unrecognizable, with diffusion model failing to preserve scene layout, object boundaries, or semantic consistency. #### 5.4.2. Avatar cut-and-paste

In the second experiment, the role of the avatar cut-and-paste step introduced in Section 4.3 is assessed. This step was introduced to preserve anatomical fidelity in human avatars. In this experiment, another set of 43,000 BCon-enhanced images is generated, this time without applying the cut-and-paste procedure. Instead, the raw outputs from the diffusion model were used directly for DNN training.

Surprisingly, the YOLOv10-M model trained on these unprocessed images, generated using the tuned values for ControlNet hyperparameters, achieves a slightly higher AP of 66.2%, compared to 65.7% obtained through the anatomically refined (cut-and-pasted) version, under the same training configurations described in Section 5.2. This small performance gain suggests that the diffusion model embedded in BCon is already sufficiently capable of generating semantically aligned worker representations for the detection task, a result attributable to structured design of the framework, including the provision of well-tuned inputs and condition controls. Moreover, the slight variance may result from minor inconsistencies introduced during cut-and-paste blending, such as mismatches in lighting between the pasted avatar and the underlying scene.

These findings imply that while the cut-and-paste step may benefit applications where visual accuracy or anatomical correctness is essential, it is not strictly necessary for achieving high detection performance. BCon users who prioritize framework simplicity or efficiency can safely disable this optional step with minimal impact on model accuracy.

## 6. Reflections and limitations

This section distills what BCon means for practice and research and candidly outlines its current constraints. The discussion first addresses implications for industry and academia, including cost, privacy, and deployment benefits, then details limitations around compute demands, avatar handling, post-hoc filtering, domain specificity, evaluation scope, task generalization, and comparisons with alternative domain-adaptation methods, concluding with concrete directions for future work.

### 6.1. Implications for industry and academia

The findings hold significant implications for the construction industry by demonstrating that high-quality, BCon-enhanced synthetic images can accelerate deep-learning development, match the performance of real-world datasets, and substantially reduce data-collection costs. Because the images are both realistic and fully annotated, they facilitate rapid training and deployment of robust DNNs for critical tasks such as safety monitoring, where improved detection enables more reliable hazard identification. The near-parity performance between models trained on BCon data and those trained on real-world images confirms that enhanced synthetic data is a viable substitute when real data are scarce, difficult to obtain, or expensive to label. Moreover, by circumventing the costs of manual annotation, BCon offers economic advantage while sidestepping privacy and logistical concerns inherent in on-site data collection.

Moreover, this study pioneers the systematic fusion of a controllable graphical simulator with a state-of-the-art conditional diffusion model to produce photorealistic yet fully annotated construction-site imagery, thereby closing the long-standing reality gap that limits the value of synthetic data in the built-environment domain. Beyond demonstrating the technical feasibility of this "GenAI-augmented simulation" paradigm, this work contributes (i) a rigorously tuned, task-specific ControlNet configuration for construction worker detection, identified through an automated GPT-4o-driven grid search, which is offered as a practical starting point for similar applications by future researchers rather than a universally optimal setting, (ii) a curated dataset of 25,600 realism-enhanced images with 2D/3D, segmentation, depth, and pose annotations preserved, and (iii) a comprehensive evaluation showing that models trained on the enhanced data outperform those trained on both baseline synthetic and size-matched real datasets. By publicly releasing the codebase, a practical and reproducible framework is provided for enhancing synthetic data realism in construction, empowering researchers and practitioners to generate and customize high-quality annotated datasets tailored to their specific computer vision tasks.

### 6.2. Limitations and future work

Although the BCon framework delivers remarkable gains in image realism and downstream worker-detection accuracy, several limitations remain, and addressing them suggests clear avenues for further research and development. These limitations span computational efficiency, treatment of avatars, overhead of post-hoc filtering, domain specificity of the study, scope of evaluation metrics, task generalization, and comparisons with alternative domain-adaptation techniques. The following discussion outlines each of these aspects in detail.

Achieving high-quality, photorealistic enhancement inevitably demands a compute-intensive framework. Enhancing a single 1280 × 1280 image using SD-XL-based ControlNet requires approximately 37 s on an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU. At peak operation, BCon, including all its components, occupies approximately 21 GB of GPU memory. Processing the entire dataset of 25,600 images consumes significant computational resources, totaling 263 h (11 days) of GPU time. Consequently, alleviating these computational demands, through any combination of algorithmic, architectural, or hardware innovations, will be essential for scaling the framework. Several avenues, such as reducing image resolution, adopting lighter diffusion models, or leveraging parallel processing, could be explored in future work to reduce computational overhead and improve scalability.

Unlike the dynamically enhanced backgrounds, human avatars in the BCon-enhanced images remain unchanged after the avatar cut-and-paste step. While this approach is adopted to prevent distortions and maintain anatomical accuracy, it does limit the adaptability of human figures compared to the rest of the image. An alternative approach could involve separately enhancing human avatars using ControlNet and then reintegrating them into the enhanced backgrounds. However, this method would significantly increase the processing time and computational resources required. Future work will explore whether this additional complexity yields meaningful benefits in realism and model performance.

Following the BCon enhancement, an automated post-hoc filter was applied to remove samples with problematic annotations or poor visual quality. Images were discarded if any bounding box covered <1% or >90% of the frame, if ControlNet had introduced unlabeled human figures, or if global illumination was excessively dark or bright. Approximately one-third of the generated images were eliminated, yielding a refined dataset of 25,600 high-quality samples. Fig. 9 shows typical exclusions: the top-row BlendCon images exhibit minimal human presence, extreme scale, and suboptimal lighting, whereas bottom-row BCon outputs contain additional unannotated humans in the background, potentially hindering effective feature learning. This intervention highlights the need for further refinement of the enhancement process to minimize such artifacts. While employing more advanced generative models may mitigate these issues, it would demand significantly greater computational resources.

*Fig. 9. Examples of images excluded during the post-hoc filtering.*
- Note: Top row: BlendCon images with minimal human visibility, inappropriate scale, poor lighting conditions, and overly close subjects. Bottom row: images from the BCon-enhanced dataset where ControlNet introduced additional, unannotated human figures.

While the underlying principles of the proposed framework have the potential to be extended to other domains, this study is limited to construction worker detection in static images within construction environments. Additional research is needed to confirm its applicability in other settings and domains where synthetic data is used but suffers from the reality gap.

Current validation relies on automated perceptual realism metrics and AP50–95 for worker detection DNNs. Future work will incorporate business-oriented evaluations to quantify the practical benefits of using BCon images. This includes conducting cost-benefit analyses to compare the expenses associated with real-world data collection and annotation versus the computational costs of the proposed framework. Assessing the return on investment for deploying models trained on enhanced synthetic data in industrial applications could provide valuable insights into the economic viability of the proposed approach.

Although the evaluation focused exclusively on BCon's effectiveness in the 2D worker detection task, extending the framework to support other computer-vision tasks, such as semantic segmentation, action recognition, and pose estimation, could further enhance its utility and demonstrate its versatility across diverse downstream applications.

Finally, to further bridge the performance gap with real-world data, future work aims to integrate complementary domain adaptation methods. Comparing BCon with other frameworks, such as adversarial training or feature alignment techniques, may reveal synergistic effects. Combining ControlNet-based enhancement with feature-level domain adaptation might potentially address unmodeled variabilities.

## 7. Conclusion

This paper presents the BCon framework, which combines the synthetic image generation capabilities of the construction-centric BlendCon framework with the image enhancement strengths of ControlNet, a neural network architecture that adds spatial conditioning controls to the generative diffusion models. BCon preserves annotation integrity by design, provides fine-grained control over realism, and does not require retraining or large paired datasets, making it both scalable and broadly applicable to construction vision tasks. This integration bridges the simulation-to-real domain gap in synthetic construction images while preserving full annotations, such as 2D/3D bounding boxes, human poses, semantic masks, and depth maps, to improve deep neural network training.

Through comprehensive hyperparameter tuning and validation using GPT-4o, confirmed by human experts, image realism is enhanced, as evidenced by substantial improvements in DreamSim score, VIEScore, CLIPScore, and FID. Notably, models trained on BCon-enhanced images achieves an AP50–95 of 65.7%, outperforming those trained on original BlendCon images (60.9%) and real construction datasets (65.6%) of equivalent size, which highlights a slight but noteworthy gain over the best real-data baseline. In summary, by increasing realism and diversity of synthetic images while preserving annotations, the proposed approach offers a practical pathway to mitigate the synthetic-real domain gap, alleviate data scarcity, and reduce the costs and risks of large-scale data acquisition and annotation.

Looking ahead, several directions emerge. First, broadening the scope beyond 2D worker detection to semantic and instance segmentation, 2D and 3D pose estimation, action recognition, and multi-object detection will test generality and help identify task-specific adaptations. Second, augmenting the framework with real-time, temporal, and 3D-aware extensions could enable realistic, site-specific, label-preserving sequence synthesis, supporting tasks such as progress tracking and behavior analysis. Third, efficiency improvements, including model distillation, mixed-precision and quantization, scheduler and step-count optimization, and parallelized rendering, could reduce GPU hours and expand practical adoption. Fourth, a model-in-the-loop synthesis paradigm, such as active learning and targeted resynthesis of failure cases, could close the quality–quantity trade-off by generating only what improves downstream performance. Finally, opportunities exist to develop construction-aware realism metrics and annotation-drift diagnostics, and to study cost-benefit trade-offs to inform deployment in safety monitoring, robotics perception, and digital-twin workflows.
